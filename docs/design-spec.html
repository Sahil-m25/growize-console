01

The distinction the whole design rests on

You asked for manager visibility, lead transfer, and a buddy system. Those are three answers to one question, and they conflict with the first fixed rule in Addendum B unless one thing is separated out first.

Addendum B fixes it in writing: one lead, one IR Associate — assigned at capture, unchanged to conversion. A transfer button breaks that rule. A secondary owner who can edit anything breaks it more quietly.

The way out is not to soften the rule. It is to notice that the rule is about custody — who is answerable for this lead, and whose name the funnel attributes to — and that almost every real-world reason you want to hand a lead to someone else is about coverage: who is allowed to act on it today.

Ananya is on leave for five days. The lead does not care. Its same-day first touch is still due, its Day-3 call is still due, and its produce pack still ships on Thursday. What it needs is someone with permission to act — not a new owner. When Ananya comes back on the 30th, nothing should have to be handed back, because nothing moved.

The rule. Ownership is set once, at capture, by the person who had the conversation — and changes only through an audited reassignment with a reason code. Everything else you wanted from a transfer button is coverage: time-boxed, logged as acted_for, and invisible to attribution.

This costs nothing to implement and buys three things at once. Managers get real visibility without becoming shadow owners. Absence stops being a hole in the SLA. And the conversion numbers stay computable, because owner_at_capture never moves under them.

Where the owner comes from

Ownership is set at capture, but who is capturing decides what happens:

Who adds the leadOwnerWhat the add screen says
An IR — any of the three seatsThem, immediately "You added it, so it is yours." No picker, no decision. They spoke to the person; they carry them.

An unassigned lead is a breach, not a state. It sits in a red card at the top of the screen the person who can clear it is already on — Leads for an IR, who takes it in one tap; My day or Leads on the Team scope for a manager, who hands it to any IR in one tap. It is red on the sidebar in both places, and it is a group of its own in Updates. The alternative — auto-assigning to whoever happened to type it — is how the IR & Growth lead ends up holding forty leads she never intended to work, which is the §4.1 failure this design exists to prevent.

The six rules that make it work

  • The owner is the human who had the conversation. Not a round-robin, and never an automatic fallback. An IR's capture is theirs the moment they save it; anyone else's arrives with no owner and waits — visibly, in red, at the top of the team scope and on the sidebar count — until a person is chosen. Auto-assigning to whoever happened to type it is the §4.1 failure this design exists to prevent.
  • SLA clocks never pause for absence. They belong to the lead. A clock that stops because someone took leave is not a service level, it is a preference.
  • Availability is the trigger, not the colour of a dot. The secondary may work a lead while the primary is marked out on the roster, or while a dated cover window is open — and not otherwise (§03b). Lateness and absence are different facts and the first version conflated them.
  • The secondary is the SLA backstop, not just the leave plan. At 75% of any SLA window — hour 3 of the first touch, hour 54 of the Day-3 call — the lead auto-escalates to the secondary owner with a push, whether or not the primary is at their desk. A tripwire that fires into a dashboard nobody is reading has not fired.
  • Cover speaks in its own name. Outbound during coverage carries a handover line — "Nikhil here, covering for Ananya this week" — so the lead is never handed a stranger with no explanation. The promise was one point of contact, not one anonymous voice.
  • Pair on segment and language, not alphabetically. The secondary has to be able to hold the conversation the primary was having. An NRI ticket at 1 crore and a 10-lakh first-time enquiry are not interchangeable books.
  • Reassignment has a closed list of reasons. Left the company · long-term absence · escalated by the lead · territory correction · load rebalancing · duplicate merge. Anything outside that list is a coverage problem wearing a reassignment costume.

Primary, secondary, and the signal that moves a lead between them

Every lead carries two names and one colour. The colour is not a mood — it is derived, every time the page draws, from lateness and cover state, so nobody has to remember to set it:

SignalWhen it firesWhat appears on the lead
● On trackNothing late, nobody covering — or the money is in and the lead is past ReservedNothing. The page stays quiet.
● SlippingOne or two days past a stage clock, or the primary is being coveredAn amber strip naming the secondary owner, with both handover buttons beside it.
● BreachedThree or more days late — or the lead has no owner at allThe same strip in red, and the lead sorts to the top of the team queue.

The strip offers exactly two actions, and the difference between them is the whole point of §2:

  • Hand over temporarily. Opens a cover window. The secondary works the lead; the primary stays owner of record; every action is stamped acted_for; attribution does not move. This is the answer to "she is on leave until Tuesday".
  • Make them primary. A reassignment. The two names swap, the change is reason-coded and written to the log, and it counts as an exception in the numbers. This is the answer to "he has left the company".
Who may press them. The primary, the secondary, or a manager — nobody else, including the BU Owner. A person handing their own lead to their own named backup is not an escalation; it is the system working. Everything else is, and goes through a manager.

An IR cannot move a lead. An IR can ask.

"One lead, one owner" would be hollow if the owner could hand it on whenever it got difficult. It would also be cruel if they could not say that they are the wrong person for it — a lead that has gone to a language, a city or a ticket size somebody else covers better is not a failure, it is information.

So the owner of record — and only the owner — can ask to reassign: a name from the roster, and a reason from the same closed list a manager would have used. A secondary does not get the control. They are there to carry the lead when the owner cannot, which is a different job from having an opinion about whose lead it should be; a request raised by somebody who is not answerable for the outcome is the start of a book being traded rather than worked. For the same reason a secondary can start and end cover but cannot make themselves the owner. The request changes nothing on its own. It appears on the lead, on the manager's day as a card of its own, and in Updates. Their manager, or anyone above, approves or declines — and an approval performs exactly the reassignment a manager would have made by hand, carrying the asker's reason into the log. A decline is recorded too, on the lead, so the answer is visible rather than silent.

02a

Custody, and where it stops being yours

Seven steps in, the IR's part is finished. They keep the record and the visibility, and lose every control — because the lead is now someone else's job.

Ownership and custody are not the same thing, and conflating them produces the two failures this section exists to prevent. If the IR keeps edit rights after the money is in, a stage can be un-ticked under Finance's feet and a signed agreement can be contradicted by a note. If the IR loses the lead entirely, the person the investor actually talks to has no idea what is happening to them — and the promise of one point of contact quietly dies at the moment it matters most.

So the answer is not one flag. It is a custodian, read off the ladder that already exists:

StepsCustodianWho can change the recordWhat the owner sees
1–5 · Captured to Investor said yesThe IR The primary, the secondary while covering, IR & Growth, Digital Everything, and every control. This is the job.
6–8 · Reserved, Fully paid, AllocatedFinance Finance, and Digital Everything. No buttons. A banner at the top of the lead saying so, and an entry in Updates every time it moves.
9 · OnboardedThe IR, again The primary, the secondary while covering, IR & Growth, Digital Everything, and the controls back. The manual makes onboarding a stage and gives it to the person the investor already knows.
The transfer to the investor databaseDigital Digital, and the Operations Lead who performs it, on Finance's request The same.
After the transferNobody Nobody — the record is closed and the investor lives in the other org The whole history, permanently.
One function, not a scatter of checks. custodian(lead) reads the step the lead is on and returns the seat that owns it, straight out of the ladder's own who column. Every gate in the product — the Mark done button, the call log, both tick lists, the handover buttons — asks that one question. Add a step to the ladder and the custody rules move with it; there is no second table to keep in sync.

What the IR keeps, deliberately

  • The lead stays on their Leads screen for life. Scope is lifetime and custody does not touch it. A rep should be able to point at the investor they brought in eight months ago.
  • They stay the owner of record. Every conversion, source and per-person figure still attributes to them. Losing the controls is not losing the credit — that distinction is the whole reason the two concepts are separate.
  • They are told, not left to check. Every move Finance or Digital makes on their lead lands in Updates (§07b). Taking away the buttons without adding the notification would just be a quieter way of cutting them out.
  • The names can still change, but only a manager changes them. Past step 7 the two handover buttons stay on the page for the IR & Growth lead, the Operations Lead and Digital, and disappear for the primary and the secondary. Reassigning a lead that Finance is actively closing is a decision, not a convenience.

Eight hours, and one rung back

Custody says who may change a record. It says nothing about when, and without that second rule an owner can quietly re-write last month the week before a review — which is how an audit log stops being worth keeping.

Anything recorded can be taken back for eight hours, and only one rung back. Long enough to fix the tap you fat-fingered walking out of a clubhouse; short enough that yesterday is history. It applies to a ladder rung, a logged touch, a reply, a material tick and a produce-pack week alike. Past the window the control is a padlock with the reason on it, and a correction is a new record rather than an edit to an old one.
  • The two rules compose, and the refusal says which one stopped you. "Recorded 3 days ago — a rung can be undone for 8 hours, and only one back" is a different sentence from "This lead is with Finance now", and a person who reads the wrong one goes and asks the wrong colleague.
  • Past the 10% advance the owner cannot undo anything at all, whatever the clock says. The rung belongs to Finance, so the window never opens for the IR — custody is the outer gate and the eight hours are the inner one.
  • Money outranks a stage correction. A rung with a payment recorded against it refuses to move until the payment is reversed or the reservation lapsed, and says so in those words.
The banner has to say why, not just that. "You cannot edit this" is how a rep learns the tool is against them. The lead reads "This lead is with Finance now. You stay the owner of record and see everything that happens — the controls belong to whoever holds it. You will get an update here every time it moves." Same restriction, opposite feeling, and it takes one sentence.
02b

Three words that must never overlap

"Transfer" was doing three jobs. A word that means three things in one product means nothing in all three.

Ask five people in this team what a transfer is and you will get three answers: moving a lead to a colleague, lending it out while somebody is on leave, and creating an investor record in the other Zoho org. Those are three different events with three different permission sets, three different audit consequences and three different failure modes — and one of them is irreversible.

So the vocabulary is fixed, and the product uses it with no exceptions:

WordMeans exactlyOwner of recordWho does it
Assign Giving an unowned lead to a person for the first time. SetA manager, or an IR taking one for themselves
Reassign Changing which person owns a lead. Reason-coded from a closed list, audited, counted as an exception. ChangesA manager; the primary or secondary only while it is still theirs (§02a)
Cover Someone else working a lead for a dated window, because the owner is marked out. Every action stamped acted_for. UnchangedThe named secondary owner, for as long as the handover says

The rail item is called "Investor transfers", not "Transfers". Two words instead of one, and the ambiguity is gone from the only place a person could stumble into it. The screen's own subtitle repeats the definition, because a glossary nobody opens is not a glossary.

What the Investor transfers screen holds

It was a queue. A queue answers "what do I have to do", and nothing else — which meant the moment a transfer completed, the record of it vanished from the only screen that was ever about transfers. It now holds both:

  • Waiting — what Finance has confirmed as paid in full and Digital has not yet performed, with who confirmed it, when, and which IR brought them in.
  • Investors transferred — every completed one, newest first: investor, ARL ID, units, amount, the source they came from, the IR who brought them in, and who performed the transfer with the timestamp. Amount is masked for anyone outside Finance, Digital and the BU Owner and Corporate Operations.
  • The totals across the top — transferred, units, raised, waiting. Raised is masked to ••• outside Finance, Digital and the BU Owner and Corporate Operations, like every other amount. This is the closest thing in the product to an answer to "how is the raise going", and it belongs here rather than in a funnel chart, because these are the only numbers that are not projections.
  • Reconciliation — fully paid against transferred. A gap means a paid investor was never created, and it must read zero in both directions.

A transferred lead leaves the leads book, so each completed row keeps its own snapshot of the investor's details. Without that, the history would empty itself every time the product worked correctly.

02c

What a button is allowed to say

The console cannot send a WhatsApp. It cannot connect a call or deposit ₹2.5 lakh. A label that claims otherwise makes people hesitate before every tap.

Almost everything in this product is a record of something that happened somewhere else — on somebody's phone, in a bank, at a farm gate. A few things the console really does: it assigns a lead, it approves an extension, it mints an ARL ID. Those are different acts and they should not look alike.

So there are two label forms, and one test that picks between them: if I tap this, does the console do the thing, or does it write down that I already did?

FormWhenWhat it reads
Past tense — a record The act happened outside the console. Tapping writes it down and stamps a time and a name. WhatsApp sent · Intro email sent · Call connected · Agreement sent · Advance received · Balance received · Paid in full · Reservation lapsed
Verb and object — an act The console itself performs it, here, now. Assign to me · Approve 15 days · Save the note · Set the next step · Start cover · Make them the owner · Release the units
Never a bare verb. "Assign" became Assign to me, "Change" became Change the step, "Remove" became Remove from the team, "Open" became Open the record. A verb with no object makes the reader supply the object, and different readers supply different ones.

The label change that turned into a feature

Writing WhatsApp sent on the queue exposed something: the button did not record anything. It opened the lead, where you then found a tick list. So the label was either a lie or an instruction to go somewhere else.

It records now. The three first-touch service levels are tappable from My day and from the ladder, each writes its own timestamp, and the first one also ticks First touch made, because that is precisely what the rung means. Consent still gates it, and it is still refused on a lead that is not yours. An IR working a list of fifteen can now clear the first touches without opening one of them.

The same test says what a queue row may promise. Where the record can be written from the queue, the pill is the record. Where it cannot — a payment, a transfer, a next step that needs a date — the pill reads Open the record, because that is honestly all tapping does.

What we are not building, and why

A generic "Manager"
Three different people manage here. IR & Growth owns conversion outcomes; the Operations Lead owns whether the machine ran; the BU Owner owns the number. One permission set cannot serve all three — and merging them is exactly how an Operations Lead quietly starts holding leads, which the manual forbids in the same sentence that defines the role.
A regional team-lead tier
Four IR seats today, all in one city. A hierarchy invented before it exists produces org-chart permissions nobody can explain in six months. Add it when a second city does.
A read-only "IR viewer" seat
Everyone in IR owns leads. A viewer seat is what gets handed to a contractor for a fortnight, and then a lead list walks. If someone needs to see the numbers, that is what the BU Owner and Corporate Operations seats already are.
Per-brand roles
Growize is the only brand running this engine. When Greenlyfe joins, brand becomes a scope on the existing ten seats — not ten more seats.
03a

Who may manage whom

A manager adds people, removes them and changes their seat — and can never hand out access they do not hold themselves.

The usual way to build this is a rank ladder: everyone gets a number, and you may act on anyone with a smaller one. It breaks the first time two roles are peers with different reach — which here is immediately, because Finance sees payments that the IR & Growth lead does not, and the IR & Growth lead sees a team queue that Finance does not. Neither is above the other.

So the rule is not a ladder. It is a subset test, run against the screens themselves:

You may grant a seat only if every screen that seat reaches is a screen you already reach. Nothing else is consulted — not a rank, not a title, not who reports to whom. Add a screen to a seat and the ceiling moves on its own, with no permission table to remember to update.
SeatMay grantMay not grantWhy the line falls there

The second half of the rule, which is easy to miss

Limiting which seats you may hand out is not enough on its own. If Jhalak may grant "IR Associate", and Sahil is sitting there as Digital, then without a second check she could demote him to IR — a seat well inside her reach — and quietly take the Digital chair out of play. So the same subset test also runs against the person:

  • You may act on someone only if their current seat is also inside your reach. Anyone above it appears in the members list with a lock, no Remove button, and no chip in the seat picker.
  • Both checks live in the handler, not the markup. Hiding a button is a courtesy; the function refuses regardless, which is what makes it a permission.
  • Every seat change and every removal is logged — who, whom, from what to what. The People screen is the highest-consequence screen in the product that is not System.

The access grid: pages down, capabilities across

Thirteen rows, one per screen, each with the specific things that screen can be allowed to do — see it, change things, export, bulk import, see other people's, change seats, mark availability, load the sheet, record payment, send documents, perform the transfer. Every one is a toggle. The seat fills them in; changing the position rewrites the grid and clears any overrides, which is the honest behaviour — a new position is a new answer, not a patch on the old one.

  • You can never grant what you do not hold. A capability you lack is drawn locked with a reason on hover, and toggleCap refuses it regardless of what the markup shows. That is the same subset rule as seats, applied one level finer.
  • Losing the page loses everything on it. Turning off see it clears the row; turning on anything else turns see it back on. There is no such thing as editing a screen you cannot open.
  • An override is marked. A row that no longer matches the seat preset carries a changed tag, and one button resets the person to their seat — so "why can she do that" always has a visible answer.
  • Every toggle is logged with the person, the page, the capability and the direction. The access grid is the highest-consequence screen in the product that is not System.

Removing someone does not orphan their book

Removal is a deactivation, never a delete. The person stops being able to sign in and drops out of every assignment picker, but they stay in the members list marked left, and they stay the owner of record on every lead they captured until someone is reassigned onto it — because owner_at_capture is what every source and conversion figure is built on. Their leads turn red the moment they go inactive, which puts them at the top of the team queue with a named secondary already on each one, ready to be made primary in a tap.

03b

The roster, and what being out actually does

Somebody marks each person in or out. That one toggle is what decides whether a secondary owner may touch a lead — so it has to be a record, not a habit.

The design already had a secondary owner on every lead. What it did not have was a trustworthy answer to when the secondary is allowed to act. The first version used the red-amber-green signal as the trigger, which was wrong on inspection: a lead can be late because the owner is at their desk and behind, and it can be perfectly on track while the owner is in Kerala for a week. Lateness and availability are different facts.

So availability becomes its own record, kept by a person, and the rule becomes plain: the lead belongs to the person it is assigned to. The secondary can work it only while that person is marked out, or while a dated cover window is open.

The four things it changes, and the one it does not

  • The secondary can work their leads — they move from being visible in their book to being in their day, and the edit controls unlock. The owner of record never changes.
  • Their open leads with no secondary at all turn red and sort to the top of the team's day, so an absence with no plan is visible within a draw rather than at the next review. A lead that has a named secondary goes amber, not red — the secondary is the cover, and the console should not call a covered lead a breach while simultaneously putting it in somebody's day.
  • "Until they are back" becomes a real date, taken from the roster rather than guessed.
  • The roster tab shows what needs cover — every open lead of an absent person that nobody is carrying, with whether it even has a secondary named.
  • And the one that does not change: service-level clocks. They belong to the lead, not to the person. A clock that stops for leave is a preference, not a service level — and the whole reason the secondary exists is that the lead does not care who is on holiday.

Cover now asks how long

Handing a lead over used to assume a fortnight. It now asks, with five answers — today only · 3 days · 1 week · 2 weeks · until they are back — and writes the end date onto the lead. Cover is per lead, not per person, so handing one lead to a colleague does not silently give them act-as over a whole book. The strip on the lead shows who is covering and until when, and an End cover button that works whether the cover came from the roster or from that one handover.

03c

Teams, and the manager as a ceiling

A seat says what a job is. A team says who a person answers to. The second one turns out to be the cheaper control, because it is the one an organisation already maintains.

Ten seats and a per-page capability grid gave a precise answer to what can this person do and a hopeless answer to who keeps it right. Somebody has to sit in front of a fourteen-row grid every time a person joins, moves or is promoted — and nobody does, which is how a permission model rots into "everyone has everything because it was easier".

So the org chart carries the weight. A team is a named manager and the people appointed under them, and the manager is the ceiling on what those people can reach. Move somebody to a different manager and their access changes on its own, with nobody opening a grid — which is the point of appointing them there in the first place.

Three layers, and they never mix. The manager bounds which pages somebody reaches — you cannot reach past whoever you sit under. The seat decides what they may do on the pages they reach. And a person may only hand out something they hold themselves. Each layer answers a different question, and keeping them apart is what stops the model collapsing into a single ladder of rank.

Why the ceiling is pages and not permissions

The obvious reading of "they can never hold more than their manager" is to intersect everything — every page and every capability. That reading is wrong within one move of the seed data: Harsha runs Finance and reports to Arvind, the BU Owner, who may look at a payment and may not record one. Under a full intersection Finance loses the ability to record a receipt, which is Finance's entire job.

The distinction that survives is reach versus right. What a manager bounds is the set of pages — the surface of the company somebody is allowed to see, which is exactly what an appointment decides. What somebody may do once they are on a page comes from the seat, because that is a statement about the job and not about the reporting line. Finance records receipts because Finance records receipts, not because their manager delegated it downward.

A clash is named, not silently absorbed

Once the ceiling is real, a seat can ask for a page its chain cannot reach. The console does not quietly drop it, and it does not quietly grant it either — it refuses the pairing. Appointing somebody under a manager who cannot reach what their seat needs is rejected with the exact list of pages, from both directions: the manager picker greys out the impossible managers, and the seat picker greys out the impossible seats. Any clash that survives from imported data is marked on the team card and on the person, in words, rather than showing up later as a screen that will not open.

ChangeWhat it does on its own

Change somebody's seatThe grid resets to that seat's preset. If the seat reaches past their chain, the change is refused with the pages named.
Remove a managerTheir reports are re-appointed to the manager above, in the same write. Nobody is left under somebody who has left.
Promote the managerEverybody under them gains the reach as well — which is usually right, and is at least visible on one card rather than spread across a grid.
A toggle that changes nothing is worse than no toggle. Every capability on the grid is now read by the handler that does the thing — revoking "Change things" on Leads actually stops the edit, and revoking "Reassign an owner" stops the reassignment without touching the edit. The ones that had no implementation behind them (Export, Bulk import) were removed rather than left as theatre: they cost a manager attention, wrote a line into the audit log, and left them believing they had revoked something.

Personal or team, chosen in the sidebar

The instinct is to give a manager one big list with a filter on it. It is the wrong shape, and it fails in a specific way: the moment Tasneem's own leads are mixed into a queue of fifteen, her own work becomes something she has to filter for — so she stops doing it, and the personal book quietly rots while she supervises. A queue you have to search is not a queue.

The first attempt at fixing that built a separate Team page with its own tabs. It worked, and it was wrong: the same two lists existed twice under different names, so "where do I look" became a decision instead of a reflex, and every rail item had a shadow. The scope is not a different screen. It is the same screen, pointed at a different book — which makes it a control, and the right place for a control that governs a section is on the section itself.

  • My day and Leads carry their own Personal / Team choice in the rail. Open either and two sub-rows appear underneath it, each with its own count.   • Only a seat with a team sees the choice at all. An IR's rail is unchanged; there is no disclosure, no chevron and nothing to decide. A control that is always set to the same value is not a control, it is clutter.
  • Personal means primary or secondary or currently covering, for the lead's whole life. The drawer: one place for everything that is not the main thing

Every screen in this product had the same failure hiding in it. A picker opened inline — the next-step chips, the recovery action, the add-member form — and everything below it jumped down the page. You then chose from a list while the thing you were choosing for had moved out of view. It is a small cruelty repeated forty times a day.

One drawer, one Escape key, and the page never moves. Detail opens in a panel on the right. On a screen 1360px or wider it docks as a third column and the content shifts left to make room — so the empty space beside a reading column becomes the place detail lives, rather than dead air. Below that width it slides over with a scrim and behaves as a proper modal dialog: focus moves in, Tab is trapped, Escape closes, focus goes back to the control that opened it.
  • It is a registry, not a component per screen. Fifteen drawers — next step, forecast, log a touch, history, notes, material, produce pack, money, last call, details, owners and cover, ask to be moved, the reservation clock, a recovery action, add a member — and any screen opens one by name. Nothing else has to know how a drawer works.
  • A door is a toggle. Clicking the same door again closes it. Navigating away closes it. Switching seat closes it. A drawer whose record has gone out of reach closes itself before anything renders.
  • It answers the question you asked about the cost of asking. Nothing loads until it is opened, and each drawer reads one slice — the history of one lead, the log of one lead. Behind a real database that is one query when the door is clicked, instead of eleven on every page view.
  • Docked is not modal. On a wide screen you can still work the page beside it, and the markup says so — aria-modal is false, there is no scrim, and Tab leaves the panel. Cross the width with a drawer open and it re-decides which of the two it is.

Every one of those already existed somewhere: the load is on Availability, which lists the same people; the call-backs are the dated next actions; the backup pairs are on each lead. A screen whose every fact is a copy is a screen that will eventually disagree with the original. The one figure worth keeping — open leads per person, with the red count — moved onto Availability as a bar.

Finding a lead, which is the first act of tracing it

A list of nine is scanned. A list of ninety is searched, and a book that cannot be searched teaches people to keep their own list somewhere else — which is how a CRM becomes a parallel truth. So Leads opens with a search box, and the box reads every field a person actually remembers a lead by: the name, a few digits of the phone, the e-mail, the city, the owner, the event they were met at, the stage they are on, the next step. Two words narrow to leads matching both.

  • Every filter composes with every other. A stage, an owner (on Team), one of the hygiene exceptions — no next action, overdue, going cold, forecast unevidenced, no consent — and a source, all at once, and the search on top. The header counts 8 of 15 so the cut is never invisible, and one control clears the lot.
  • The rail is the filter. The strip that used to sit under the table is now a column beside it: the book by stage, the exceptions, the forecast in units, the sources and — on Team — the owners, each row a count and a bar. Tapping a row applies that cut. "Three going cold" is never a number you then have to go and find; a zero is not a row you can tap.
  • The empty state says which cut hid the lead. "Girish" is in your book, but the other filters hide it is a different sentence from Nothing in your book matches "Girish", and a manager on their personal scope is told how many matches sit in the team's book, one tap away. An IR is told nothing about leads outside their book — the search is gated by the same rule as the page.
  • A masked field is not a searchable field. A seat that sees a phone as +91 990 ••• ••• cannot confirm the number by typing it; the phone and the e-mail join the search only where the seat may read them.
  • / or Ctrl+K finds a lead from anywhere the seat has a Leads screen, and the search survives opening a record and coming back — tracing is a loop, not a one-shot. A seat switch drops it, so nobody inherits a search they did not type.
  • A number elsewhere lands on the leads behind it. A hygiene tile on Numbers, a stage on the morning's book card, a source row — each opens Leads already cut to exactly what was counted, on the scope that holds it. Finance is the exception: its list is Reserved and beyond, so its Numbers do not pretend to link.

Density: fitting it in one screen

Simple and spacious are not the same thing, and the first pass confused them. Cutting words made the product simpler; it also left a lot of air, and air costs scrolling. Every screen fits one viewport with nothing important below the fold.

The frame is one screen; the columns inside it scroll. On a desktop the app is exactly the viewport — the page itself never scrolls, so the rail, the header, the filter chips and the tab row never move under the hand. Each column takes whatever height is left and scrolls only when its own content is taller than that. No screen carries a hand-tuned pixel number. An earlier version did, and it got the worst of both: short columns scrolled early while a dead strip sat under them. Below 900px the rail stacks on top and the page scrolls normally, which is what a phone wants.
  • Two columns wherever content is two things, three where it is three. The lead page runs the ladder and Finance down the first column, the call log and the produce pack down the second, and the tick list, history and details down the third — 1,780 pixels tall in the first version, 900 now, with nothing removed. Numbers, People, Plan, System and Activity got the same treatment.
  • Timestamps moved onto the line they belong to rather than under it. Ten ladder rows, ten lines saved.
  • Base type down a step, padding down about a third — 14px, tighter card and cell padding, smaller stat figures. Still comfortably above the minimum for a tablet held at arm's length.
  • Long lists scroll inside their card, not down the page — and a list that is the only thing on its screen gets the whole fold. Verified against a seeded book of 120 leads across every seat, scope, tab and group; nothing pushes past the fold in either colour scheme.
  • Width earns its keep. A queue in a 1,400-pixel column is a narrow strip of text with a thousand pixels of nothing beside it. My day and Updates now run the list at a readable measure with a context column next to it — first touches outstanding, leads with no next step, the reservation clocks ticking down, and where the book stands. Leads runs the table beside the rail that reads and filters it. Payments puts the shelf and the clocks beside the receipts, Events puts what is coming beside what ran, and Documents puts the outbox beside the compose form — and shows one column, not an empty second one, to a seat that cannot send. Every one of those is a fact the screen already owed the reader.
  • A day has a horizon. Under today's queue sits The week ahead — seven rows, one per day, always, whether or not anything is dated on them: next steps, balances falling due, expected full payments, and who is rostered out that day. An empty row is information; it is the day you can take. Plan the week opens the full horizon.
The fourth pass: nothing opens in place. The three passes below made the frame one screen, the columns fill it, and the cards fill the columns. What none of them fixed was the page growing under your hands when you tapped something. That is what the drawer is for, and it is why the lead page could go from eleven cards to two: the other nine did not need deleting, they needed a door.
The third pass: the last card in a column fills the column. The frame was one screen and the columns filled it, but the cards inside did not — so every short list left a dead strip under it: Updates using ninety pixels of seven hundred, a notes card capped at a hundred, a history capped at five rows, an activity feed capped at 286 while the page below it sat empty. One rule fixed all of it: a card marked to fill takes whatever height its column has left and scrolls inside itself, never shrinking below a readable minimum, so the column still scrolls when its content is honestly taller. History now shows the whole trail, notes the whole thread, the activity feed the whole day. Still no hand-tuned pixel number anywhere — the harness checks 1,096 seat-by-screen-by-viewport states in both themes and nothing overflows.
What we removed, and why it counts as design work. The first version put KPI tiles on an IR's home screen, a provenance chip on every number, an explanatory paragraph under every card heading, and five rail items in front of a rep — ten in front of a manager. All of it was true and none of it was theirs. Data provenance is an admin's problem; funnel counts are a manager's; the rationale belongs in this document. A rep is now down to five rail items — My day, Leads, Updates, Add lead, Activity — and the words on them fell from about 1,200 to 400. Updates is the only one added back since, and it was added because §02a takes controls away: a restriction without a notification is just a quieter way of cutting someone out. What is left on the Today screen is a name, what is late about it, and one button.
07a

The plan, and who may move a number

Every tripwire, every red dot and every "needed" bar in the product is derived from the plan. It belongs on a screen, at whatever duration the business actually runs on.

208 units on 8 acres by 31 March 2027 is not a fact about the world. It is a decision, and the moment it changes — because a month was lost, or the raise moved — every service level, every funnel bar and every capacity trigger in the product is wrong until someone finds the constant and edits it.

So the plan lives on a screen, is typed rather than nudged, and everything else reads from there. The one non-obvious rule is that a period is a window, not a label: it has a start and an end, and every figure on the row is scoped by it.

  • It reconciles, visibly. 15 baseline + 193 planned = 208 units, ₹52.00 Cr, 8 acres by 31 March 2027. When someone edits a target and it stops reconciling, the line goes red and says so, rather than quietly disagreeing with the manual.

A rep does not need a target screen; they need the target already inside the queue in front of them, which it is — every "late", every amber and every red on their Today list is one of these numbers doing its work. The seats that can open the Plan see the same page; the ones who own a figure get an input on it, the rest get the figure alone. A target nobody can see is not a target, but a fifth rail item on a rep's screen is the thing this whole design has spent its time removing.

07b

Updates, and how not to build a firehose

Everyone gets one screen that answers "what happened to my leads while I was not looking" — generic on arrival, specific only when you ask.

A notification feed is the easiest thing in a product to get wrong, and the failure is always the same: it starts as a courtesy, becomes a wall, and ends up ignored — at which point the one notification that mattered is lost inside four hundred that did not.

The brief here was explicit: generic, not very specific until needed. That is a design constraint with real teeth, and it produces four rules.

RuleWhat it prevents
A group says what happened and to how many leads, and nothing else until you open it The wall. Five lines answer "is anything happening", which is the question 90% of visits ask. "Moved on without you — 3 leads" is the whole notification.
One row per lead, never per event The arithmetic that makes a feed meaningless. Twelve produce-pack ticks on one lead is one line, not twelve. The first version of this screen said "257 leads" for four.
Only what someone else did Being told about your own work. Your actions are on Activity, where they belong.
The last seven days, and no further A backlog masquerading as news. Anything older is history, and history has its own screen.

Deliberately not in Updates: your own overdue work. That is the Today screen's entire job, and duplicating it here would give two screens the same red count and teach people to ignore both. Updates is for what other people did; Today is for what you have to do.

07c

The five controls the manual names

Each of these is a sentence in the manual with no field behind it. A control with nowhere to be recorded is a control that does not exist.

The manual's operating rules are short enough to quote. Five of them describe things the console could not hold, so five things were added — each one narrow, each one with an owner, and each one visible as an exception when it is missing rather than buried in a report.

1 · A dated next action on every active lead

The manual: "Every active lead must always have one owner, one next action and one due date," and, as a control, "No active lead without stage + next action/date."

The next action, and the forecast below it, are also the one pair of fields that do not follow custody. When the 10% advance moves the record to Finance, Finance confirms the money and the IR keeps the follow-through — which is what the manual's Table 9 and Table 18 say, and it is the only exception in the custody model.

So the next action is a closed list plus a date and, where somebody agreed one, an hour. "Call me after six" is a promise with a time in it, and a step carrying only a date turns that into a whole day of guessing — the missed call-back is the cheapest lead in the engine to lose. The picker is a dropdown, a calendar and a clock, in a drawer, with quick chips beside them; the hour is what puts the item in the right place on the day it falls, and it is what My day sorts the queue by. It sits at the top of the lead — the first thing on the page, above the lifecycle. Its absence is not a blank field; it is a red card that says the record is out of hygiene, a row in the Leads list you can filter to, and a "now" item at the top of that owner's day. Before the first touch lands, the three first-touch service levels are the next action, so a freshly captured lead is not counted as a breach — the manual puts this control on qualification and nurture, which starts once the touch is made.

2 · Commit, Probable, Pipeline

CategoryWhat it meansWhat it needs
CommitInvestor-specific evidence supports the expected full-payment date Evidence in the investor's own terms, and a date. Missing either is flagged on the lead and counted on Numbers
ProbableCredible, with a defined next step and timing — not yet Commit The same two things. The bar is the evidence, not the optimism
PipelineEarlier stage. Useful for coverage, never for claiming achievement Nothing. That is the point of having a third category
The FY rule is enforced, not written on a wall. A reservation counts toward the year's forecast only when the expected full-payment date is on or before 31 March 2027. A Commit dated after that is shown as outside FY and rolled up separately; one with no date or no evidence rolls up as unevidenced; and once the money is actually in, the date stops being a forecast and is read as the fact it became, marked realised — four different states, never merged, so nobody has to remember the rule to obey it. Dates carry no year on screen, so the code reads a forecast date forward and a due date to the nearest calendar year; getting that wrong is how a date in April quietly becomes last April.

3 · Sellable inventory

The manual's hardest control is one line: "No unit can be reserved or allocated twice," next to a hard gate that says the commercial team must not sell inventory that cannot be delivered. The console had no concept of a shelf at all.

It has one now: released is what the farm interface has confirmed deliverable, and allocated, reserved and free are computed from the book — counting a transferred investor once, not twice. The check runs on any receipt that walks a lead onto the shelf, the advance and the balance alike; when nothing is free the payment is refused and the person is sent to the Plan, where the shelf is. Money cannot outrun the ladder either: nothing is recorded against a lead the investor has not yet said yes to, and an advance is never written over a confirmed full receipt. Finance and Digital hold released inventory; nobody else can move it.

4 · The reservation extension

Balance is due in 30 days. Beyond that, the manual's authority table names exactly one approver: the Growize BU Owner, on an IR request with Finance status. A lapse forfeits ₹50,000 per unit and puts the units back on the shelf.

A clock runs only while a balance is outstanding: the moment Finance records the balance the card is gone, along with any request still attached to it, because there is nothing left to extend. While it runs, a reserved lead carries days left, red at three, and three controls that match the three roles: the owner, the secondary, a manager or Finance may ask for 7, 15 or 30 days; only the BU Owner may approve or decline; only Finance may release the units and record the forfeit, once the hold has actually passed and no request is still waiting on a decision. The confirmation shows both figures — what is forfeit and what goes back under the standard refund rule — before the button is pressed. An approved extension extends the deadline it was granted against; it never resets the clock to today. Every one of those is logged with a name.

5 · A recovery action on every amber and red

The manual: "Every Amber/Red item has one owner, one recovery action and one date." The scorecard it describes wants the top five surfaced with those three fields filled in.

Numbers' second tab opens on exactly that block: the top five amber and red lines, each with owner, action and date, and a count of how many are still bare. Every amber or red line further down the page carries the same three fields inline, and a line without them is red — because an unowned amber is the failure mode the rule exists to prevent. The bands are the manual's own: green on or above trajectory, amber 90–99%, red below 90%, and the same bands colour a plan period. The plan's total also goes red outright when the units still to sell exceed what the farm has released, which is the same table's other clause.

07d

The horizon: a week and a month ahead

A queue answers "what now". It cannot answer "I am out from the 25th — what has to move?", and that is the question somebody asks before they book leave.

Everything in this product was, until now, about today. That is right for the person working a list and wrong for the same person planning a fortnight. So My day takes a horizon switch — Today · This week · This month — and reads the same book forward instead of down.

Nothing new is stored. The dates already exist: the next action's date, the reservation's 30-day hold, the expected full-payment date. The horizon simply asks each day what falls on it.

Pull it earlier is the one action offered from here, and it is deliberately not a date picker. If the owner is away when the item falls, it moves to the working day before they go; if they are not, it moves two days in. One tap, the obvious answer, and the change is logged like any other. Somebody who is about to take a week off can walk the month, pull the four things that would have gone stale, and hand over the rest — which is exactly what the manual means by a handoff being complete before it is needed.

07e

Touchpoints, and the question they answer

An email is not one event. It is sent, then sent again, then a third time — and whether the investor ever came back is the only thing that tells you if they want it.

The first version stored one timestamp per channel, which is enough to know whether the service level was met and nothing else. It could not tell you that four emails had gone out and none had been answered, which is the difference between a lead worth working and a lead worth closing.

So a channel holds a list. The first entry is what the service level reads — that part is unchanged. Every one after it is another attempt, recorded by hand, one tap, from the queue or from the ladder.

Where it lives, and how one gets logged

It was a card of its own. It is now inside the lifecycle, on the rung it belongs to — because "we texted three times and called twice before they came back" is the story of First touch made, and putting it anywhere else asks the reader to join two things up themselves.

Logging one is a drawer with a dropdown in it: what happened, when — a date and a time, defaulting to now — and Save it. One attempt per save. Send the second WhatsApp on Thursday, come back and log it then; the drawer stays open and keeps the channel you were on, so a run of attempts is a run of saves rather than a form to re-open. Below the picker is every entry already logged, newest first, each with its own date and its own take-back.

  • A count and a span per channel, on the rung — "Intro email sent ×3, 19 Aug → 26 Aug" — so the shape of the effort is legible without opening anything.
  • Reply received is one of the options in the same dropdown. It is the only entry that is not an attempt, and everything logged after it counts as a fresh run.
  • A backdated entry is allowed; a future one is not. You can log Tuesday's call on Thursday, and the log sorts it into place. You cannot log a call that has not happened — the count only means something because every entry is something that already went out.
  • Touches since they last came back — and where that reaches four with nothing returned, the card turns red and says what the manual says: keep working a no-reply lead, but the next attempt should change channel or change the offer rather than repeat itself. That state has its own filter on Leads, called Going cold.
Nothing here is automatic, and the wording says so. The console does not send, so it cannot count for you. Every attempt is a person tapping a button after they did the thing — which is why the labels are in the past tense (§02c) and why the count means something when it says four.
07f

Sections, one at a time

A wide screen is not a licence to run two stacks of cards down it. Two stacks have no reading order between them, which is what a split screen actually feels like.

Numbers, System, Events and Investor transfers were each two columns of unrelated cards. Nothing was wrong with any single card; the problem was between them. Two independent stacks give the eye no order — it starts at the top left, finishes a card, and then has to choose, every time, whether the next thing is below or across. That choice is small and it is made forty times a screen, and the sensation it produces is the one the word "split" describes.

The replacement is not narrower columns. It is a summary that stays put, a row of section chips, and two or three full-width cards on one topic at a time. One reading order, top to bottom, and the width used for the thing that actually wants it — a table with eight columns, a bar with a long axis — rather than for a second column of something else.

08

Adding and changing data in bulk

The spreadsheet question, answered properly: it is the right tool for one job and the wrong tool for the other, and the difference is not a matter of taste. Then the event sheet, which is that right job.

Creating a lead is append-only. Changing one is read-modify-write. That single distinction settles the whole question.

A new lead is a new row. Nothing existing is at risk and the worst failure is a duplicate — visible, countable, fixable later. At hundreds a week a sheet genuinely beats any form, because people paste twenty rows from a campaign export in one motion.

Changing a lead is the opposite. To mark one Qualified you read what it says now, change it, and write it back. But the sheet went out on Monday and comes back Thursday, and in between the record moved — someone else edited it, a workflow fired, a lead was reassigned. Uploading Thursday's sheet silently overwrites Tuesday's truth and leaves no record of what it destroyed. You cannot audit it afterwards, because the evidence is what got overwritten.

DoorUse it forCreatesChangesSafeguard
Tablet captureOne lead at a time, at an event yesnever Offline queue; consent mandatory

What the import actually does

  • Maps once, remembers. Column mapping is saved per import type, so the second campaign export is one click rather than fifteen dropdowns.
  • Validates before it writes. Mobile parseable to E.164, source inside the picklist, consent column present. Nothing commits partially — you fix inline or exclude the row.
  • Flags duplicates, never merges them. Matching is on the normalised mobile. Two leads sharing a number is a real thing that happens — a couple, an office line — so it is shown side by side and you decide per row. And note which failure review cannot catch: matching on phone merges two people who reuse an office number, but it also splits one person the day someone types a digit wrong. A false merge appears side by side and gets caught; a false new lead appears alone and quietly inflates your count. Only a minted Lead_Ref catches that one.
  • Assigns an owner on the way in. Pick one IR for the whole file, or take the owner from a column. A lead with no owner has no clock, and a lead with no clock is the one that dies.
  • Refuses to import consent it was not given. A bought list with no consent record is not a lead list under the DPDP Rules; it is a liability. The importer asks where consent came from and stores the answer against every row.
Volume is not the constraint, and will not become one. The Zoho upsert API takes 100 records a call, so a few hundred leads a week is three or four calls. The daily allowance is 51,000 API credits — 50,000 plus 1,000 per licence — and a week of leads costs a few hundred. The Bulk Write API (25,000 records per CSV up to 25 MB, 500 credits a job) is built for migrations and is oversized here. Build for the upsert, and build for calls in the plural: a 198-row import is two of them, not one atomic write.
Why the whole design waits on one field. The Leads module has no external-id field. The only things Zoho will let you match on are Email, Phone, Mobile and PAN, and none of them is safe as a key — which is the entire argument for adding Lead_Ref. It costs one field and ten minutes, it is the upsert key forever, and it is also what a salesperson quotes when they ring you about a lead.
The sheet keeps one job. At an event with a queue in front of you, twenty rows pasted in one motion beats any form ever built — so keep the Google Sheet as an offline capture buffer: validated dropdowns matching the Zoho picklists exactly, a generated Lead_Ref per row, an "ingested on" column the job fills, and the row locked after that. One way, and it stops.

Use Workspace accounts on it. Google then records the author of every cell edit with a timestamp, visible in version history and unforgeable in the way a self-declared "Added by" column never is. Carry that author through to Zoho as Captured_By — otherwise attribution stops at the sheet and the CRM cannot answer who sourced a lead, which is the exact question the BU Owner-network source exists to answer.
And it stops being one person's job. "They send me the sheet and I upload it" puts you personally in the path of every lead the business receives, several hundred times a week. The scheduled pull is the highest-leverage thing on this page: nobody emails a file, nobody waits for you to be free, and a person off sick does not stop the pipeline.
One field a file may never change: the owner. A correction import that reassigns leads is a reassignment wearing a spreadsheet costume. Ownership moves through the audited path or not at all — otherwise the exception metric quietly stops counting, and so does attribution.

Then one question — who carries them: round-robin across the people who staffed the event, all to one person, all to me, or leave them unassigned. Then one button.

  • Every row lands tagged to the event. That is the entire reason the sheet is per event rather than per week — §09a's cost-per-qualified figure is only computable when the tag is set at capture, and retro-tagging a week later is guesswork dressed as data.
  • Duplicates are skipped, never merged. A match on mobile means the person is already in the book with an owner and a history. Silently merging two records is how an IR loses a lead they were working.
  • Incomplete rows stay on the sheet, with the reason written beside them, so the person who filled it in can fix it. They are not dropped and they are not half-imported.
  • A sheet loads once. After that, corrections go through the lead — because by then the console is the record and the sheet is a historical document (§08 above).
The gap between "rows on the sheet" and "will load" is the finding. 38 rows, 31 loadable, 4 duplicates, 3 missing consent — that is not an import error, it is a report on how the evening was run. It belongs on the event page next to the cost.

Logging a touch without asking anyone to log

Your real question — how do you get five people to record what happened, when, and how, in a format a report can read — has a three-layer answer, and only the middle layer involves a human.

Ask a person to log every touch by hand and they won't. Not through carelessness — because it is twenty seconds against every conversation, and the twenty seconds always loses.

LayerWhat it doesHuman effort
1 · Machine captures WhatsApp through the BSP webhook, calls through cloud telephony, email through each person's connected mailbox, webinars through Zoom. Channel, direction, timestamp, duration and delivery state all land in lead_activity with nobody typing. none
2 · Human confirms The two things a machine cannot know: what happened and what happens next. Served as a card the moment the call ends — outcome chip, objection chips, next action and date. three taps
3 · Form guarantees format Every field a report reads is a closed picklist. A free-text note exists and nothing computes from it. none

This is why "correct format" stops being a training problem. Nobody types a status, so nobody can type it wrong. The reason your picklists already contain New/ and Darft is that somewhere a human was trusted with a text field that a chart later read.

How you know each person did it

An unconfirmed touch is a state, not a habit
A call that connected and never got an outcome is an unlogged touch — it appears on that person's My Day and on the Control Tower. You are not auditing whether people "use the system"; you are looking at a list of specific events that are missing one field.
Four numbers per person, none of them subjective
Touches confirmed within 24 h · live leads carrying a dated next step · scorecard completed at Qualified · both lost fields present on close. Each either happened or did not, so a gap is coachable rather than a suspicion — and the pattern is legible: an IR at 42% on scorecards and 8% on Qualified→Reserved does not have two problems.
Three gates that make it self-enforcing
A lead cannot reach Qualified without a completed scorecard. It cannot be closed without a lost reason and a lost step. A commercial document cannot be sent while the KYC pack is incomplete. A field that blocks something people want gets filled; a field that only feeds a report does not.
Nothing is retro-editable without a trace
Events are append-only. A correction is a new row that supersedes, never an overwrite — so "contacted on the 23rd" cannot quietly become "contacted on the 21st" the week before a review.

Nothing typed that can be tapped

A unit is ₹25 lakh. Everyone knows that, so nobody should ever type 2500000 — or 25,00,000, or 25L, or 2.5, which is how a free number field actually gets filled. The interest field is four chips reading ₹25 L · ₹50 L · ₹75 L · ₹1 Cr, each labelled with its unit count underneath. Payment is three chips — advance, balance, full — each showing the figure computed from the units already on the record.

The same rule runs everywhere: outcome, objection, next step, mode of payment, lost reason, source. The only free-text fields in the product are a person's name, their mobile, the UTR, and an optional note nothing computes from. Everything else is a closed list, which is why "correct format" stops being something you train and starts being something the form guarantees.

Recording a payment

Three chips, not a number field: 10% advance, full, balance, each showing the rupee figure computed from the units. Mode is a picklist. The UTR is the only thing typed, because it is the only thing that is genuinely arbitrary. An advance is a liability and is never shown as capital.

The transfer, which is a request and not a button

When the full amount lands, Finance does not create the investor. Finance raises a transfer request, and it appears in a queue for Sahil or Jhalak, who performs it. The queue card carries the investor, the unit count and one line — payment in full confirmed by Finance, with a date. Not the amount, not the UTR: the admin is confirming that a verified record exists, not re-checking the money, and giving them the figures would only invite them to. That action mints the shared code, creates the investor and the allocation in the investor org, and stamps the same code on the deal, which stays where it is forever.

Two people, one doorway. Splitting it this way is not bureaucracy — it is the only reconciliation you get. Finance knows the money arrived; Digital knows the record was created. If those two counts ever disagree, one of them is wrong and you can see which. A single person doing both means a dropped investor is invisible until someone notices a payout that never went out.

Signing

e-Mudhra with Aadhaar OTP, with the Aadhaar field on the provider's own page so the number never touches our front end, our logs or our error reporting. What gets filed is the signed PDF and the audit trail.

In OTP mode, Aadhaar e-sign needs an Aadhaar linked to an active Indian mobile — UIDAI holds no international numbers, so a large part of the NRI pipeline is blocked. Ask at qualification, not on the day of the close. Fallbacks: a token-based Class 3 DSC (obtainable from abroad, though certifying authorities generally want passport and visa with embassy or notarial attestation), or wet signature by courier.
The legal position moved in your favour. MeitY's notification of 6 October 2022 omitted contracts for the sale or conveyance of immovable property from the IT Act First Schedule — they can be e-signed now, and your data dictionary still says otherwise. Registration under the Registration Act 1908 is a separate question and still physical where it applies.
The rule for the send API, in one line. Refuse the e-sign path if the template is a negotiable instrument (unless a cheque, or a demand promissory note or bill of exchange issued in favour of or endorsed by an RBI / NHB / SEBI / IRDAI / PFRDA-regulated entity), a power of attorney that does not empower such an entity, a trust, or a will. Both carve-outs are directional. Growize is none of those regulators, so a farm-operations power of attorney stays wet-signed. The consequence of getting it wrong is that the signature carries no statutory recognition — the paper original remains perfectly valid.

10c

What is working, and how we know

A status page that lists only failures teaches a reader exactly one thing: do not trust any of it. That is not what a status page is for.

The System screen was nine findings and a permission matrix. Everything on it was true and the overall impression it gave was false — because the ten things that work every day were not on it, and a reader has no way to tell "nine problems out of nineteen checks" from "nine problems, and who knows what else".

So every check is on the page, in one list, with the same fields: what it does, what breaks if it stops, who owns it, what would fix it, and three weeks of its state. The ones that pass are listed first, because they are the majority. A check with a clean run behind it is the reason a figure can be trusted at all, which is a thing worth putting on a screen rather than assuming.

Be exact about what the history strip is. It is not twenty-one observations — this console polls nothing. It is the check's own record: the date its state began, the state before that, and any single day somebody had to step in, drawn day by day so a change of state has a position you can point at. Deterministic, never sampled, never random, and marked demo alongside every other figure the prototype has not earned yet. The alternative — drawing a plausible-looking history — would have been the most quietly dishonest thing in the product.

Every finding names what closes it

Rebind the integration to a service account. Restart the nightly export and alert on a missing run. Add a datetime field per stage and write it on transition. Rename the field label and re-point the reports. Each one is a sentence somebody could act on this week, which is what turns a list of complaints into a build order — and it is the same discipline the twelve numbers already follow, where every unmeasurable one names the field that would make it real.

Help, which is mostly "why won't it let me"

Almost every question people ask a console like this one is a refusal they did not expect. The honest answer is always a rule somebody wrote down on purpose.

Help here is not a manual. It is two things in a drawer, reachable from anywhere with ?: what the screen in front of you is for, and the rules that decide what the console will and will not let you do. The second half is the one that gets read.

That shape follows from a decision made much earlier in this design — that every refusal names the rule that caused it rather than greying a control out in silence. Help is where those rules are collected: custody and where it stops being yours, the eight-hour window and its one rung, consent per channel, the manager ceiling, what a secondary may do and when. Sixteen questions, searchable, each answered in three sentences.

Every answer in it had to be made true first. Writing the help text was the most effective review this design has had: four of the sixteen answers described behaviour the code did not have — per-channel consent, a duplicate check on the mobile number, "only one rung back" being one rather than one per click, and an absence that clears itself on the return date rather than the day after. Each was fixed rather than reworded. A console whose help is aspirational is worse than one with no help at all.

Decisions only you can make

Each of these changes the build. None of them has an obvious right answer from the documents.

Was it right to take the target off Jhalak, Sahil and Pradeep?
You asked for the goals page to be editable by those three. The manual gives the BU target to the Growize BU Owner and nobody else, so the number itself now sits with Arvind — and with Sahil, who holds every screen as the system seat. Everything around the number stayed where you put it: Jhalak and Pradeep still set the period windows, the granularity, the funnel rates and the demand mix, and both still set recovery actions. If you would rather keep the target with them as well, it is one line — but the log will not tell you the number moved for the wrong reason, only who moved it.
Where does released inventory actually come from?
The console holds it as a number Finance and Digital type, sourced from the farm interface. That is honest and it is also a place a stale figure could sit for a month and quietly authorise sales that cannot be delivered. If the farm side has any system at all, this should read from it and the typed number should become a fallback with a visible last-confirmed date. If it does not, the number needs a standing owner and a weekly confirmation, which is a process decision rather than a build one.
Does the console write to Zoho directly, or does Zoho become a mirror?
Addendum B fixes Zoho as the system of record and the mini-ERP as an interface that writes through. That is the safe answer and it is what this design assumes. But if the leads org turns out to have one seat and no reliable API path, the honest alternative is Postgres as the record and Zoho as a reporting mirror — a bigger decision than it looks, and better made now than after the licence renews.
Is the secondary owner a person or a pool?
A named secondary is warmer and produces better cover — they already know the book. A pool is more robust at 15 people and stops one absence cascading. This design assumes named pairs, because at this size the quality of the cover conversation matters more than the queueing. Revisit at about twenty.
Whose number does the lead see during cover?
Three options: the covering person's own org SIM with a handover line (assumed here); porting the SIM, which is operationally ugly; or a single shared business number for everyone, which solves cover and destroys per-person attribution. The third is the one to be careful about, because a BSP will quietly push you toward it.
Does an unconsented lead block outbound, or just warn?
Blocking is correct under the DPDP Rules and will be unpopular in month one, when the tablet workflow is still settling. A warn-then-block ramp over the first two events is defensible. Never capturing consent is not.
When Aadhaar e-sign fails, is the default a DSC or a courier?
A remote Class 3 DSC through video KYC keeps the whole close digital but asks a 1-crore investor to obtain a certificate, which some will find odd and some will find reassuring. Wet signature by courier is slower and unimpeachable. The honest answer probably differs by ticket size — worth deciding once rather than per deal, because the console needs to know which path to offer first.
Who chases an unsigned document — the IR or Finance?
Addendum B says the IR stays the single point of contact, which argues for the IR. But the IR cannot see the document's contents, so they are chasing something they cannot discuss. This design has the IR chase and Finance answer questions, with both copied. The alternative — Finance chases directly — is cleaner operationally and breaks the one-voice promise.
Should Digital be able to change the BU Owner's seat?
The subset rule says yes, and it says so honestly: Digital reaches every screen a BU Owner reaches and one more — System — so nothing about a BU Owner is outside Sahil's access. It follows from "Sahil has more, because of the system setup". If that reads wrong — and there is a reasonable argument that the person who owns the company should not be demotable by the person who owns the software — the fix is one line, a protected-seat list that the subset test checks first. Cheap either way, but it should be a decision rather than a side effect.
Can a manager give a seat to someone outside their own reporting line?
Today the IR & Growth lead manages her own tree, while Operations Lead and Digital manage everyone. That is right at ten people. At thirty it means Jhalak is the only person who can move someone between Tasneem's team and a second IR & Growth lead's, which will become a bottleneck the week the second one is hired. The alternative is scoping People by reporting line for every seat below Digital — more correct, and more to explain.
Does a removed person's book auto-reassign, or wait for a human?
This design deactivates the person, turns their leads red, and leaves the reassignment to a manager working the team queue — one tap each, because a secondary is already named. Auto-promoting every secondary the moment someone leaves is faster, and on a real book of forty leads that is forty taps saved. It also silently rewrites attribution across a whole book with nobody looking at it. Speed against a reviewable record; at this size the record is worth more.
Who can see the objection list?
It is the most useful coaching artefact in the system and also the most uncomfortable — it shows which IR hears "price" four times as often as anyone else. This design puts it with the IR & Growth lead. Making it visible to the whole team is a culture decision, not a permission one.
